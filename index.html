What this is

Every word you use is
a salvaged older word.

Etymøn is an open-source map of where words come from, and a projected installation built on top of it. It began in 2018 as an undergraduate capstone project at Bilkent University: scrape the world's etymological dictionaries, fold them into one graph, and see whether the shape of that graph says anything the dictionaries do not.

The map holds 3,161,104 etymological relations between 2,970,656 words in 255 languages, assembled from four sources that had never been joined up before. The installation takes a few hundred paths through it and makes them readable: a word is picked, and its ancestry is dived for, one language at a time, until it reaches a form no one ever wrote down.

The argument is in what surfaces. Almost every word we have coined for this technological moment turns out to be a much older word pressed back into service, and usually a physical one:

Englishtext
Old Frenchtexte
Latintexereto weave
Proto-Indo-European*teks-to weave, to fabricate

A text is a thing woven. So is a textile, and so is subtle — sub tela, under the warp of the loom. Code was a tree trunk split into tablets. Noise descends, by way of Old French, from Latin nausea, seasickness, and so from naus, a ship. The divan you sit on is named for a Persian account-book, which is named for an Old Persian word for writing, which is a Sumerian clay tablet. And hallucinate, the word we reached for when machines started inventing things, is Latin alucinari: to wander in the mind.

The installation

An ocean of words.

A projection that runs unattended, forever — and a chart you can read. The field above is it: sixty thousand real words from 224 languages, moving on the same two sine waves the 2018 site used for its landing screen.

A word's position is an argument, not decoration, and it works at two scales. Which region a word lies in is its language family — and the regions are arranged by how much they actually exchange words, by multidimensional scaling on the corpus's own edge counts. So Romance sits against Germanic because that is where the borrowing went, Turkic borders Semitic and Iranian for the same reason, and the invented languages end up alone offshore. Nobody chose the arrangement. Where it sits inside that region is resemblance: character n-grams embedded with t-SNE, so spelling neighbourhoods form and languages clump without being told to.

The third axis is time. A word lies at the era of its language, so every region is layered — living languages floating, Latin and Ancient Greek well under, the reconstructed proto-forms resting on the floor. Colour is family, dimmed and pushed toward blue with depth, the way water actually takes the red out of things.

Every twenty seconds the piece dives. A word is chosen, its ancestry rises out of its own region and turns to face the room, holds long enough to be read, and sinks back. It works through eleven movements and begins again.

Or you can drive it

Move the mouse and it stops performing: the cursor returns, words light up under it, and clicking one opens its ancestry beside its Wiktionary entry — the panel the 2018 app had, with its Jump to word button still in it. Press / and you can search 2,562,401 words, every one in the corpus that has a real chain, held locally in sharded files so it works with the network unplugged. Leave it alone for a minute and it goes back to running itself. There is a microphone too, if you want to just say a word.

Three kinds of light

The 2018 project had a button marked hallucinate, six years before that word meant what it means now. It ran a character-level LSTM over the corpus and produced things shaped like etymologies that never quite held a meaning. The idea was right and the model was too small. That button is still here, rebuilt — which raises the question of how an audience is supposed to tell an invention from a record. The piece answers it by giving each its own light, and saying so on the wall:

Attested

Asserted by one of the four source corpora. Drawn as a steady filament.

Conjectured

Absent from every corpus, and proposed. The scrapes are patchy — token stopped dead at Middle English, abyss had no ancestry at all — so a language model was asked for the next real step, and 478 of these fill the gaps. Standard scholarship, but because no source asserts it, it is drawn as a proposal: a pulse travelling down the edge.

Dreamed

Invented. No such word has ever existed. Built to the right shape — laryngeals and ablaut for Proto-Indo-European, triconsonantal roots for Proto-Semitic — so that it sits at exactly the same depth as the genuine reconstructions and cannot be told apart by position. It shimmers, drifts off its own colour, and evaporates. Confined to the closing movement: spread across the whole score it stopped being an argument and became wallpaper.

Given the machine's own vocabulary, the dreaming turns inward. It gives prompt a Proto-Indo-European ancestor meaning that which is drawn out of another's mouth; token, the mark a finger leaves in wet clay; and hallucinate, to wander a great distance while standing still. None of them are real. All of them are the right shape.

It has a soundtrack, as the original did: The Meaning of Words by Savfk, used under CC BY 4.0. The six-bar equalizer in the corner still turns it on and off.

The dataset

Four dictionaries,
one format.

Sources

  • Etymonline — Douglas Harper's Online Etymology Dictionary. English, with dense Latin, Greek and Old French ancestry.
  • Nişanyan Sözlük — Sevan Nişanyan's dictionary of contemporary Turkish, and its Arabic, Persian and Old Turkic sources.
  • Starling — the Tower of Babel comparative databases; Turkic and Abkhaz-Adyghe reconstructions.
  • Etymological Wordnet — Gerard de Melo's machine-readable set derived from Wiktionary, which supplies most of the breadth.

Most connected languages

Two things the 2018 pipeline got wrong

Both were found by rebuilding it, and both are fixed in installation/pipeline/. First, the master file stores every relation alongside its own inverse, so 6.7 million lines describe about 3.1 million facts; they are now collapsed into one canonical direction. Second, and worse, the Etymonline scraper wrote human-readable language names while every other source wrote ISO codes — so Latin, Old French, Greek and PIE, the four most important languages in the whole dataset, were dangling strings that joined up with nothing. Resolving them recovered 34,790 descent relations and 87,000 references that had been silently dropped for seven years.

The data is in data/ as CSV and TSV, and the full graph is in the repository's releases. Everything is open; the dictionaries it derives from belong to their authors, who are credited above.
